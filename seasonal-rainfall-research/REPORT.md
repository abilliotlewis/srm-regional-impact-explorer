# Seasonal clustering and predictability of extreme rainfall in the southeastern United States

## Research report and reproducible pilot

**Status: exploratory pilot, not a completed regional attribution or forecast study.** Prepared 29 October 2026. The analysis uses actual observations. It does not establish the number of independent storms affecting the southeastern United States, and it does not assess the requested full set of drivers or models.

### Abstract
We examined quality-flagged daily precipitation from six NOAA GHCN-Daily stations and a November Niño 3.4 sea-surface-temperature series for winters ending 1979–2025. We defined station-specific daily extremes as precipitation above the 99th percentile of wet days in December–February (DJF) during winters ending 1979–1998, and merged exceedances separated by no more than three intervening non-exceedance days. Baton Rouge had nine episodes in 47 winters, all in separate winters. The mean count was 0.191, sample variance 0.158 and variance-to-mean ratio 0.826. A plug-in Poisson simulation did not indicate excess variance (one-sided Monte Carlo p = 0.894). At five other selected stations ratios ranged from 0.863 to 0.996. Rare counts, imperfect declustering, station selection, nonstationarity and multiple comparisons limit inference. In 17 rolling test winters, Baton Rouge had zero winters with two or more episodes; a November Niño 3.4 Poisson model scored worse than a constant Poisson baseline on Brier score (0.00209 versus 0.000262). With no positive test outcomes this comparison is not a meaningful evaluation of rare-event discrimination. This pilot cannot answer whether identifiable regimes improve forecasts of region-wide multiple-storm seasons.

## 1. Question and hypotheses
The target is P(N ≥ k | information available before a season), with N the number of distinct rainfall-producing systems affecting a specified region during that season. H1 asks whether count variance exceeds the stationary Poisson expectation; H2 whether physical climate predictors explain between-season variation; H3 whether dependence or overdispersion remains conditional on these predictors; H4 whether predictors improve out-of-sample probabilistic forecasts. H1–H4 are separate questions. A mixture of independent Poisson processes with seasonally varying rates can produce overdispersion without a storm causing another storm. Conversely, a lack of overdispersion does not establish independence.

## 2. Literature synthesis
Tuel and Martius (2021, 2022) study clustering on days-to-weeks and how climate modes relate to three-week extreme-precipitation counts globally. Their 2022 paper uses contemporaneous indices, and its explanatory relationships are not automatically out-of-sample seasonal forecasts. It reports substantial spatial differences, with especially high model fit in parts of the tropics, and often lower fit outside them. It does not establish this project's southeastern independent-storm result. Tuel and Martius (2021, Switzerland) links clustered precipitation with extreme discharge, motivating successive-event impact studies. Zscheischler et al. (2020) define temporally compounding events as one form of compound hazard, but multiple rainstorms alone do not demonstrate compounded impacts.

Battula et al. (2025) identify six synoptic patterns associated with southeastern US extremes during 2001–2019; storm-type composition and forecast performance differ by pattern and season. Their cool-season high-IVT pattern has better precipitation forecast skill than a warm-season convective pattern. This is evidence about prediction of individual-event rainfall, not seasonal prediction of multiple independent events. Zhao et al. (2017) similarly characterize extreme-precipitation patterns across the United States. Mukherjee and Mishra (2021) investigate southeastern extreme rainfall and atmospheric rivers. The ENSO winter study in Atmosphere (2022) uses weather types to examine circulation pathways; ENSO's effects vary by season and location. E3SM studies document sensitivity of simulated ENSO–extreme precipitation relationships to model resolution and ENSO structure, so physical or model relationships should not be assumed universal.

Kholodovsky and Liang (2021) offer a spatial-temporal threshold-clustering method useful for defining event footprints. Davis and Mikosch (2013) review serial extremal dependence measures, including the extremal index and extremogram. Declustering reduces duplicate threshold exceedances but does not prove resulting storms are independent. Extreme-value and point-process techniques require thresholds, dependence assumptions, and uncertainty assessment.

**Unresolved contribution.** Distinguishing multiple separate, spatially tracked storms from multiple gauge hits, quantifying seasonal count distributions and conditional risk, and validating pre-season forecasts jointly for Gulf and inland regions remain meaningful questions. Existing work covers major elements of the idea, so novelty depends on the actual benchmark, storm identity, mechanism and forecast evaluation, not on asserting clustering itself is new. Published studies differ in geography, timescale, storm definition, datasets and whether they study concurrent or forecast predictors. This limits direct comparisons and does not by itself establish contradictory findings.

## 4. Completed pilot methods
### Coverage and episode definition
The six fixed USW stations are Baton Rouge (USW00013970), New Orleans (USW00012916), Jackson (USW00003940), Mobile (USW00013894), Atlanta (USW00013874), and Tallahassee (USW00093805). They were selected for accessible, geographically spread records, not via an objective sampling design. Verify precise coordinates, airport relocations, observation time and metadata before publication. Analysis is at each station independently, not a count of storms across the region. USW records are not proof of homogeneity.

Winter year 1979 means December 1978 through February 1979. The last is winter ending February 2025; 2025 is the most recent complete calendar year at the time of this study. For each station, estimate a fixed wet-day (at least 1 mm) DJF 95th, 99th or 99.5th percentile from winters ending 1979–1998. Extreme days strictly exceed the threshold. The primary 99th percentile is fixed before the 2009–2025 forecast test. Consecutive exceedances belong to the same episode when they are separated by at most three intervening non-exceedance days. Sensitivity uses one and five days. Count per winter; require at least 95% nonmissing, quality-accepted days. A missing day between two extremes splits an episode; it can still cause missed extremes, and no exposure adjustment was applied. The procedure can both merge distinct systems and split one long-lived storm. The use of runs variants is not equivalent to a second physically tracked storm-identification approach.

### Statistics and forecast design
We calculate sample mean, variance, variance-to-mean ratio (VMR), and empirical frequency N ≥ 2. For each station and setting, simulate 20,000 equal-length Poisson samples with rate equal to the observed mean; the one-sided simulation tail is the fraction with simulated VMR at least as large as observed, with plus-one correction. This is an exploratory plug-in test, not a bootstrap confidence interval or a test accounting for trends, parameter/threshold estimation, serial dependence, coverage or multiple testing. It is weak when counts are small.

Baton Rouge pilot forecasts for winters 2009–2025 use expanding historical samples. The baseline is Poisson with mean fitted to prior winters. The conditioned model is Poisson regression with November Niño 3.4 and a fixed ridge penalty of 0.5 on the slope. Each model estimates P(N ≥ 2) = 1 − exp(−λ)(1 + λ). Brier and binary log scores are calculated on the held-out seasons. The 1979–1998 precipitation threshold precedes every forecast test, but predictor revisions, fixed 1981–2010 anomaly centering and the fixed regularization choice mean this is not a fully operational hindcast; centering is an affine shift absorbed by the unpenalized intercept. No model tuning, uncertainty interval for score differences, or comparison with a trend-adjusted baseline was performed.

Baton Rouge's 0/47 empirical rate is not proof that repeat extreme winters are impossible: the approximate 95% one-sided binomial upper bound is 6.2%, assuming exchangeable independent winters. The stationary fitted Poisson gives P(N ≥ 2) about 1.6%, conditional on its estimated mean, without parameter uncertainty. Its station 99th-percentile threshold is high, about 4.24 inches in a reporting day. Baseline thresholds are estimated from a limited number of wet days; their uncertainty is not included.

Baton Rouge sensitivity at the 95th-percentile threshold (59.90 mm) is 0.957 episodes/winter, VMR 0.861 and 12/47 winters with ≥2 episodes for both the one- and three-day gap. With five intervening days, it is 0.894, VMR 0.790 and 10/47. The 99.5th threshold gives 3 episodes total across 47 winters. The full sensitivity table is provided. These are exploratory specifications, not a family of independent tests, and they say nothing about summer, the broad Southeast, or physically independent regional storms.

### Forecast pilot
In the 17 held-out Baton Rouge winters, observed N ≥ 2 occurred zero times. Brier scores: constant Poisson 0.000262; November Niño 3.4 conditioned 0.002091. Binary log loss: 0.01607 and 0.02854, respectively. Both scores favor the baseline in this particular set of all-negative outcomes. Low Brier scores here largely reflect rarity, not proof of useful forecasts. Calibration, a meaningful reliability diagram, skill uncertainty and assessment of positive cases were not possible with this sample. Relative risk by climate phase and its uncertainty were not estimated.

|---|---|---|
| H1: overdispersion | Inconclusive for the research question | Local winter VMRs are below 1, and the Baton Rouge plug-in p is 0.894. This is not evidence that regional independent storms never cluster. |
| H2: climate-regime dependence | Inconclusive | One ENSO predictor was fitted, but no inference or robust multivariable analysis was conducted. |
| H3: residual dependence | Not tested | Conditional dispersion and interarrival diagnostics are absent. |
| H4: predictive skill | Inconclusive | Baseline outscored ENSO in 17 all-negative test seasons; there were no repeated-event cases to evaluate. |

## 6. Interpretation and limitations
The absence of detectable station-level excess variance is a finding about this sparse pilot, not a general meteorological conclusion. Sampling extremes locally at the 99th wet-day percentile yields very few events and is poorly suited to estimating multiple-event seasonal probabilities. A spatially defined region can capture more storms, though its answers depend on minimum footprint, storm splitting, and area. Adding stations without tracking would falsely multiply a single storm. Selection of the region, thresholds, predictor list and metrics before confirmatory analysis is essential.

The pilot does not quantify trends, Gulf versus inland differences, tropical-cyclone contributions, spring/summer/fall, multiday precipitation, maps, circulation composites, mechanism decomposition, confidence intervals for fitted models, cross-dataset consistency, negative-binomial models, hierarchical models, or forecasts with archived real-time predictors. Station thresholds and some November data in the earliest in-sample fits use information that would not have been available at that historical issuance date; reported held-out forecasts begin after the fixed threshold baseline. This is not a publication-ready regional finding.

## 7. Detailed plan to complete phases 2–4
1. **Construct domain and observations.** Primary geography: Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and Florida, with a fixed coast/inland partition. Use CPC daily gauges from 1979 through 2025 after verifying historical/realtime product consistency; compare a common 1981–2025 PRISM window and an independently QC-filtered GHCN subset. Record filenames, hashes, version, time conventions, masks, coverage and downloads. Use fixed 1981–2010 thresholds for descriptive study and training-only thresholds for fully rolling predictions, or specify an initial fixed historical baseline before all test years. Score forecasts only after that baseline.
2. **Define regional storms in two ways.** (A) Daily station or grid exceedance footprints, connect adjacent cells across days using overlap, displacement, reasonable storm-speed limits and predeclared merge/split rules; count unique event IDs at the region level. (B) Track precipitation objects using lower-threshold contiguous rainy areas plus extreme cores and synoptic continuity. Manually audit split/merge cases and compare event count agreement, duration and attribution. Temporal runs of 1, 3 and 5 clear days remain sensitivity analyses, not evidence of independence. Carry events across season boundaries before assigning them once by peak date or greatest impact. Test 95/99/99.5 wet-day percentiles, fixed mm thresholds and 1/3-day accumulations. Correct for valid-area changes rather than treating unavailable cells as dry.
3. **Define population and sample size.** Primary seasons DJF and JJA, analyzed separately, and coastal vs inland domains; validate whole-region and state results as secondary. Prespecify a practical active threshold using training data (for example N ≥ 3 or the training upper quartile), and publish event rates. Aggregate years only within comparable seasons; never mix different climatological seasonal means into a stationary Poisson test. Assess statistical power by simulating plausible rates, effect sizes, autocorrelation, missingness and declustering effects; label simulations explicitly.
4. **Baselines and inference.** Fit A: stationary Poisson separately per region/season; B: time-varying Poisson with predeclared smooth trend and exposure offset where appropriate. Simulate under each fitted null and refit the entire threshold/declustering/model pipeline within bootstrap replicates where feasible. Quantify dispersion, tail probabilities, waiting times, event-pair correlation and residual autocorrelation, with uncertainty from year-block or multiyear-block resampling. A negative-binomial model tests rate heterogeneity, not the physical origin of clustering. Account for multiple regional tests and exceptional years.
5. **Climate and mechanisms.** C: parsimonious pre-season ENSO, Gulf SST anomaly, and selected circulation indices, avoiding highly collinear terms; D: negative-binomial equivalent; E: constrained random season effect or latent weather-regime model only if sample size supports estimation. Separately evaluate retrospective DJF/JJA 500-hPa height, 850-hPa moisture flux, IVT, precipitable water, jet latitude, blocking and soil moisture. Fit frequency, duration and conditional event rate for independently defined regimes; avoid treating extreme-rainfall days as independent and include compositing uncertainty with season blocks. Distinguish associations, physical mechanisms and causality. Classify TC-linked storms with track, timing and rainfall footprints; rerun excluding them with matched observation coverage.
6. **Evaluate forecasts.** Use expanding or blocked rolling origins with all processing, thresholds where estimated, standardization, predictor selection and tuning confined to training folds. Use pre-season information only; use archived forecasts or observations available at issuance, accounting for data release lag and revisions. Compare against stationary and trend-aware baselines using count log score, Brier and binary log scores at prespecified k, calibration, interval coverage, and positive-season performance. Report bootstrap intervals of paired skill differences and effective independent sample size; do not report reliability curves with bins too sparse to interpret. Benchmark Poisson, negative binomial and appropriate hierarchical models; state when sample size cannot identify model complexity.
7. **Visual outputs.** Generate event-count time series, histograms with predictive uncertainty, dispersion estimates by region, map and uncertainty, circulation composites, predictor relationships, conditional probability curves, calibration and out-of-sample skill, plus sensitivity plots only after their underlying analyses are performed. The included PNG is an exploratory station result, not a regional map or physical composite.

## 8. Recommended follow-up studies
1. Build a validated Southeast regional storm catalog using footprint continuity and compare its counts with simple temporal declustering. Deliverable: an open event catalog with explicit split/merge uncertainty.
2. Separate cool-season favorable-regime frequency, persistence and within-regime extreme probability; test whether ENSO affects counts through one or more pathways.
3. Assess tropical versus non-tropical storm sequences and the sensitivity of regional multiple-event risk to track attribution and landfall location.
4. Compare genuine pre-season forecasts against climatology and nonstationary baselines using a prespecified event threshold and archived predictors, with pooled regional hierarchical estimates only if supported by data.
5. Connect successive storm sequences to river-flow or soil-moisture antecedent conditions to measure actual compounding impacts, distinguishing hazard counts from flood risk.
